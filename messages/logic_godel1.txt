Principia Mathematica (Whitehead and Russell, 1910-1913)

DESCRIPTION

    The Principia is a three-volume work on the foundations of
  mathematics.  It represents a school of thought known as logicism.
  Superficially, logicism argues that mathematics can be built from
  logic and set theory.  The Principia begins with the logic of
  propositions, a True-False or two-valued propositional calculus,
  which is encapsulated in five propositions and one rule.

    The propositional calculus uses two primitive connectives:
        ~ -- logical negation, a unary operator which inverts the
             the truth value
        ∨ -- disjunction, a binary operator which returns True
             both of its arguments are false.
  The five axioms are stated in terms of disjunction and material
  implication (a defined operator) and, along with the rules of
  modus ponens and substitution of variable yield a functionally
  complete system which characterizes the two primitive operators.

REMARKS

    The Pricipia's definition of material eqivalence in terms of
  disjunction fails for intermediate values in many-valued
  Łukasiewicz and Gödel-Dummett algebras.  For the Łukasiewicz
  algebras, we note that, in the expression '~p∨q':
     if both of the variables take on intermediate value, then
     both terms and thus the sum will be intermediate in value.
  For the Gödel-Dummett algebras, note that:
     If the variable 'p' has an intermediate value, then the
     first term will be True.

    DeMorgan's Laws fail in Gödel-Dummett algebras because, as
  the intuitionistic propositional calculus, negation represents
  impossibility.  Conjunction and disjunction cannot be defined
  in terms of one another in these algebras with more than two
  truth values when using only logical negation
